1What changed, in one paragraph

We used to send the AI one question at a time: every question paid for its own full exploration of the matter documents. Now we send it batches of eight related questions and it answers them all from one read of the documents. Because the questions share their homework, the whole form fills faster, cheaper — and, unexpectedly, more accurately, because the AI can see when questions are two sides of the same coin (tick one box, leave its siblings blank) instead of answering each in isolation.

2Test 1 — the Mitchell matter (49 form fields, 11 documents)

A synthetic family-law matter with a vendor-supplied answer key: 49 fields of a court form (checkboxes, dates, addresses, names).

Why the old way scored worse, in plain English: forms have groups of options where exactly one may be ticked (“Is the party Aboriginal? / Torres Strait Islander? / Neither?”). Asked one at a time, the AI happily answered “yes” to every option — each answer looked right on its own. Batched, it decides the group once and the contradictions are impossible.

The trap that matters: the matter file contains a tidy chronology document whose dates were deliberately scrambled. On run 1 the AI trusted it and got the cohabitation date wrong. After the “summaries are second-hand” rule, it instead trusted the letter where the other side’s solicitor accepted the true date in writing — which is exactly what a careful human would do.

4Safety rails added along the way

Two failure modes showed up in stability testing, and each now has a mechanical guard — not a polite request in the prompt, but a check the system enforces:

  • The lazy batch. Occasionally a batch declared documented facts “not found” after a shallow look. Now, before any batch of answers is accepted, every negative answer is audited against the documents automatically (a free text-index probe, no AI call): if the corpus contains the question’s own wording — “date of birth”, “lawyer’s code” — the negative is rejected back with the exact passage to read.
  • The invented value. One run answered an address that appears in no document. Quotes are now mandatory for any answer that states a value (names, dates, amounts, addresses), and quotes are machine-verified against the source text — a fabricated value can no longer be submitted silently.

6Honest caveats

  • Perfect runs are the norm, not a guarantee. Across repeated fresh runs we see ~99% per-field accuracy, with roughly two runs in three scoring perfect. If a workflow needs every run perfect, the answer is consensus mode: run each batch twice in parallel, auto-accept agreements, re-ask the rare disagreement (≈2× cost, same wall time). Designed, not yet built.
  • The question tuning was informed by observed misses. Everything we added is public form structure (option lists, answer shapes) — never matter facts — but the clean generalisation test is a fresh unseen test set using these patterns from day one.
  • Long answers are graded by an AI judge (17 of the 90 on the big set), because “the same answer in different words” can’t be string-matched. Judge-scored items were spot-checked by hand.

7Where this ships

  • All of it is on main and published to PyPI as rnsr 1.0.0a4.
  • One command per matter: rnsr answer-csv --corpus <folder> --questions <csv> --fast-ingest --batch-size 8. Ingest is cached; re-running a matter resumes from its checkpoint at zero cost.
  • 270+ automated tests; the batching machinery, the negative-answer audit, and quote verification are all covered.
